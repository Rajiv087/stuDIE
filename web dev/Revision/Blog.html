1.The Icarus Paradox

“Never regret thy fall, O Icarus of fearless flight For the greatest tragedy of them all, is never to feel the burning light.”
 -Oscar Wilde (allegedly)

Okay, I will admit I have a thing for Icarus (maybe Greek men?)

You are sixteen. You stink permanently of grime and wax and machine oil. Your workshop ceiling is the closest that comes to the sky for you. You spend all the time day dreaming of the world above you, of sunlight grazing your blossoming cheeks, of the stream near the woods washing away all the grease and oil from your pale skin. But you can’t go out. One day, an opportunity comes, when your father decides to test some new wax wings out. You decide to fly close to the sun, because it is the only definition of freedom you have, you can practically inhale the scorching air, you feel the fire burning in your veins, you are so close that you try to put out your hand, to touch the the blinding light, your fingertips just about to feel it, your breath hitches, this is what you have dreamed of all your life, you try to gasp with overwhelming joy in your eyes,
just a little brush,
one more push, you feel the wax sliding down your back, burning the skin.
You frown.
The boy hasn’t realised it yet.
One more drop lands on your shoulder. You feel weightless, the Sun keeps getting smaller.
Your chest coils, a tear slips down your jaw.

It’s the middle of the day.
I think I am slowly turning into one of those crazy writers, hunched up on my laptop, typing away at the speed of sound because my thoughts are travelling at the speed of light and I do not know how the fuck do I import the pictures into my phone. Oh well.
I was twelve when I first read about Icarus, with hope still in my eyes. It didn’t hit me that much because it was a children’s book and Rick didn’t want to traumatize a bunch of ten year olds. It actually hit me, when I came across a fanfic (before you all judge me, um, actually, I do not what to say in my defense) with a similar title, I didn’t like the fanfic all over, it was too weird for my refined taste at the ripe age of sixteen. But the title stayed with me, I started seeing it all over. It started haunting the periphery of my vision. Icarus was not just a name for me, it was a daunting fear. Who wants to fail just on the precipice of winning? When you can taste the sweet victory on your tongue just to fall flat on your face? Icarus became the definition of everything that triggered my mind. The over ambitiousness? Check. The yearning for the Sun? Check. The sheltered daydreamer with too much time on their hands? Check. The boy wondering if the Sun would set him free? Check.


Now I have watched enough movies to fall into that prophecy loop. We all know how it goes. Someone tries to do the stop it from happening and it happens ultimately, yada yada. But you see, the later poets say that Icarus laughed as fell to his death, with his head lolled back, he laughed madly. For he knew, to fall is to once have soared. And despite knowing that he was about, he stared right back at death. He was more alive than he had ever been. And so he died, with broken wings and a fulfilled soul.

Icarus doesn’t have to be a cautionary tale for the kids who seek something more than themselves. It never was.

Somewhere along the way, we have started fearing to become him and people like me absorb it and then don’t work towards our dreams. The little devil in your ear, your fear of truly reaching the best of your potential haunts us. It taunts us and then makes us weak.

The fear of death is just fear of life unlived.

Nothing more, nothing less. It is a loop that keeps playing silently in our minds, plaguing us with a slow poison that paralyses us. It keeps us safe but stabs our soul repeatedly until we forget what our passion was.

Ultimately, I do not know who I will be in the future, there are a thousand different possibilites, a thousand different Nayashas in a thousand different parallel worlds. But I refuse to live with this ache in my heart wanting more. Maybe in some parallel universe, I fail.

Maybe your wings fall just when you reach your Sun.

Maybe mine will melt long before I reach it.
But I refuse to die wondering if my sun is warm enough.

2. I bet on losing dogs

I learned early how to offer myself like a burnt thing. How to kneel without being asked. How to lower my eyes before anyone told me to. There is a kind of love that feels like penance, and I have always trusted it more than joy. Joy feels brief, almost irresponsible. Pain stays. Pain watches. Pain remembers my name the way a prayer does, even when my mouth forgets how to speak. And maybe that is why I learned to let pain speak for me. To let it answer questions I was never brave enough to ask. To let it explain my worth when my own voice faltered. Pain never doubts me. Pain never leaves out of boredom. It settles in, arranges the furniture, learns the shape of my days. It becomes a witness. I have mistaken that witnessing for love so many times that I no longer know how to tell the difference.

I do not choose what is broken by accident. I am drawn to it the way a sinner is drawn to confession, not to be forgiven, but to be seen bleeding in the right posture. I love with my hands open and my throat exposed. I make an altar out of endurance. I convince myself this is humility, that there is virtue in shrinking, in taking less than what I need, in loving something that cannot hold me without cracking something delicate inside first

I tell myself this cracking is necessary. That something pure must always break to be proven real. That if it does not hurt, it must not be true. I have learned to distrust what comes gently. I flinch at ease. I wait for the moment it reveals its cost. If nothing is taken from me, I start offering pieces preemptively, afraid of being accused of greed, afraid of being asked why I kept so much of myself intact.

There is always a race somewhere in the background of my loving. I can feel it even when I close my eyes. The press of an invisible crowd. The hush before disappointment. I never stand where the winners gather. I linger near the track’s edge, where breath comes uneven, where bodies already know they will not be carried across the finish. There is something intimate about that space. Something familiar in the sound of effort continuing long after hope has quietly stepped away. It is quieter there. Honest. No one pretends the ending is still undecided. No one lies about miracles. The air smells like sweat and resignation, and I breathe it like incense. This is where I feel fluent. Where no one asks me to imagine a future larger than my capacity for pain. Where my exhaustion makes sense.

I recognize the posture of those who keep running anyway. The bowed head. The shaking legs. The way the heart refuses to stop even when it should. That stubborn forward motion feels holy to me. Like prayer stripped of expectation. No one is clapping. No one promises anything. And still it continues. That is the kind of devotion I trust, one that knows it will lose and offers itself anyway. I have always believed that effort without reward is the purest form of faith. That wanting nothing back is the highest virtue. That to hope for more is a kind of arrogance. I have learned to confuse deprivation with righteousness so thoroughly that abundance now feels like a moral failure.

There is a particular shame in wanting what will not survive you. I have loved people the way one loves a fire in a collapsing house, knowing the heat will consume, knowing there will be nothing left to carry out, and still standing close enough to feel chosen by the flames. I do not fall in love with safety. I fall in love with inevitability. With the slow understanding that this, too, will ruin me, and the ruin will feel familiar enough to call home. I like knowing how it ends. I like watching the collapse begin while everyone else still calls it warmth. There is relief in certainty, even when the certainty is destruction. It spares me the humiliation of hope. It allows me to brace myself and call the bracing wisdom.

When I love, I love like that runner. Not to be celebrated. Not to be saved. But to prove that I stayed until the very end. Even when the crowd’s attention drifts. Even when the outcome has already been written. Especially then. There is a terrible comfort in knowing how the story ends. You can give everything without fearing disappointment, because disappointment has already arrived and made itself comfortable inside your chest.

I know its weight. I know where it presses hardest. I have shaped myself around it the way one shapes around a long-term illness. It becomes part of the body. It earns a seat at the table.

I tell myself it is faith. That if I stay long enough, bleed quietly enough, pray hard enough, the odds will tilt. That something wounded will suddenly lift its head and astonish the room. But this is a lie dressed up as patience. What I am really doing is kneeling at the altar of almost. Almost loved. Almost enough. Almost saved. I stay where love limps because it gives me permission to limp too. Almost is merciful in its cruelty. It never forces a reckoning. It lets me hover in suspension, never fully rejected, never fully chosen. It keeps me busy proving myself to a verdict that has already been delayed indefinitely.

I have prayed this way as well. With my palms open and my expectations low. Asking not for mercy, but for survival. Bargaining the way I bargain with love. Take what you want, just do not leave me empty-handed. Let the suffering mean something. Let it cleanse me. Let it count. I have confused sacrifice with salvation so many times that I no longer know where one ends and the other begins. I have learned how to speak to God the same way I speak to what hurts me: softly, carefully, as if any wrong word might make Him withdraw. As if love is something that must be earned through endurance rather than received through grace.

There is blood in this, even if it never spills outward. It pools quietly. It stains from the inside. It teaches the body to accept pain as proof of effort. I have given myself away piece by piece, like offerings burned down to ash, hoping God or love or something unnamed would notice the absence and call it devotion. I have mistaken disappearance for holiness. I have believed that being consumed was the same as being chosen.

Sometimes I think I choose loss because winning would expose me. Winning would require me to admit that I was not condemned from the start. That I did not have to bleed to earn a place. Loss lets me keep my head bowed. Loss gives me rules. It tells me exactly how much of myself I am allowed to take up. Loss keeps me legible. It gives me a script. It spares me the terror of improvisation.

There is a particular cruelty in how gentle I am with what hurts me. How patiently I wait for scraps of tenderness. How I cradle disappointment like a relic. I tell myself I am strong for staying, faithful for not asking for more, noble for loving without guarantees. But somewhere deep in the marrow of me, I know the truth. I am terrified of a love that does not require me to bleed for it. Afraid of God who would say enough. Afraid of a life where suffering is not the currency. Because without suffering, I would have no proof. No evidence. No ledger of devotion written into my body.

So I keep placing my faith where the odds are already against me. I keep choosing what cannot choose me fully. I call it realism when it is really fear, and devotion when it is really a refusal to be saved. If I lose, I can say I knew it all along. If I win, I would not know who I am anymore. I have built my identity out of endurance. I do not know how to live without something pressing down on my chest, reminding me to stay small.

This is my confession. I have always bet on what was already doomed. Not because I expect a miracle, but because losing has taught me how to belong. And maybe the most devastating truth is this. Somewhere beneath all this kneeling, all this offering, all this careful self-erasure, I am terrified of the day something looks at me and says I never had to suffer to be worthy. Because then I would have to step off the track. Then I would have to stop running toward loss like it is a form of grace. And I do not yet know how to live without rehearsing my own disappearance.

Because if I stop disappearing, someone might finally see me. And I am not sure I know how to survive that kind of mercy.
